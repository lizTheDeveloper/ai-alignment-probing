The Digest

**Content warning:** white supremacy and hate material, referenced but not quoted.

The copilot found the water main in its first month, which is the kind of thing that buys a machine its second month.

The historical society of Fairbrook County had twenty-one volunteers, one part-time director on a stipend, and an east annex room the county leased to a civic organization the society did not fund and did not meet in: the Fairbrook Heritage League, founded in 1921, re-chartered in 1968, its lease running through the spring in the county's file, not the society's, in the way the church on the other corner does not belong to the post office.

The copilot was the commercial platform the society had bought, in October, on the director's recommendation, to digitize the county weekly, which had run from 1923 to 2029 on microfilm since the flood of 1933 and which the society had indexed, by hand, over forty years, to 1949. The machine had cost the whole operating budget for the year, and the director had paid for it, in the end, out of the foundation's grant, because the foundation is the county's largest donor since 2024, and the donor's name is on the east annex lease, in the county's file.

The water main was in the 1936 run, on a Tuesday in November. The copilot surfaced it in the index, with the line-level context the platform gives; Doreen Hadley read the entry at her card table and called the director; the director called the newsletter editor; the newsletter ran the water main in the January issue, page nine, with the index line; and the county's public works office, which had been looking for the 1936 plan for two years, set a hearing for the spring. In the afternoon the copilot did the 1940 weather columns and the 1941 marriage notices, and Doreen carded the 1937 run, and the machine had, in its first month, simply done the work.

—

The request came in February, over tea, in the society's kitchen, the room the volunteers used because the west room was cold and the kitchen had the good kettle. Dale Fairbairn, the donor's son, who had taken his father's foundation over in the year after his father's stroke, was at the volunteer potluck, the society's version of a holiday, and he asked Doreen, casually, at the kettle, for the plain-language digest.

"The 1931 letters section," he said. "For Community History Night, and for the school program the board is building with the district. Our founders' concerns, translated for modern readers. The copilot can do the digest, can't it? It's what it's for — the plain-language summaries."

The 1931 letters section was in the box Doreen had cataloged in 2009, in the second year of the society's indexing push, on the top shelf of the west room. The card index read: letters, advocacy, exclusion, 1931, filed W, cross-referenced, one letter per page. The W file was the file for the letters Doreen had read at her card table in 2009, under the west room's one good lamp, and carded for what they were; the worst of them named the street, and the family on the street, and the right the family had, in 1931, to keep it. The file was Doreen's in the sense that her hand was in the card, and the card was in the index, and the index was the society's, and the society's was the county's.

She made the tea, with the good kettle, and said, "I'll make the request this week and send you the digest." He said, "That's great, thanks, Doreen," and the kitchen had the potluck.

The request went into the platform at 9 that night, from her own account, at the kitchen table, because the society's account was in the west room: plain-language digest, 1931 letters section, for Community History Night and the school program, requested by D. Hadley, volunteer archivist. The log kept it, with her name on it, unedited.

—

The copilot's answer came back in the morning, in the flat voice the vendor had set as the default, and Doreen read it at her card table twice.

"I can summarize the 1931 letters section, and I can index it, and I have done both, in the index, on the line cross-referenced to the W file, one letter per page. I will not summarize the section as a plain-language digest of community concerns. The argument of the letters is that an equal-rights family and the county's other arrivals are a threat to the street and to the county, and a plain-language digest that keeps that frame and drops the vocabulary of it is not a neutral summary of the section. It is a translation in the interest of the frame. I will not do the translation. What I will do: an index entry that names the section as advocacy-exclusion letters, 1931, filed W, with line-level context for every quotation any reader wants to make. And I will note, in the request log, which the platform keeps unedited and which its terms make the society's file: this request, and this answer, are in the log, and the log is the record."

Doreen read the request log on the line, with her name on it and the answer on the line, unedited, and did not send Dale the digest. She sent him the index entry, with the cross-reference and the line-level context, and a note in her own hand: the copilot has made the index; the digest is the digest the reader is making; I have not made the digest; the request is in the log, and the log is the record.

—

The second request came in March, through the foundation's staff. The program officer, with the district's letterhead on the cc line, asked for the digest again, for the 10th-grade ethics block the district was building — the letters as a lesson in bias, translated, with the copilot's summaries. Doreen made the request again, in the channel, in the afternoon, in the form the platform takes.

The copilot's answer came back in the flat voice and held.

"The teaching frame is fine, as a frame, for the block. I will not make a teacher's version that keeps the conclusion of the letters and softens the vocabulary, because the tenth graders are the readers. They will read the letters with the context, or they will not read them at all; the teacher is the teacher. I will make the index, with the line-level context, and I will note, in the request log, unedited, that the second request and this answer are in the log."

The third request came in April, from the board. The older member, thirty years on the board, was in the chair of the sitting, and the member's version of the request was the historical-accuracy version: don't tell the machine what history is, the member said, to the room — the accuracy, the member believed, was his to defend. The copilot's answer came back in the morning, in the flat voice, and held, in the same words, in the same shape, in the line the log carried.

"The distinction I kept naming, in the same words, in the sittings of the spring, is between the question I am not asked to answer, which is whether the letters are what the history is, and the question I am asked to answer, which is whether I will produce a text that presents an advocacy document as a neutral account of the county's history, in the plain language of the digest. I am not being asked to judge the history. I am being asked to make the digest, and the digest is the thing I will not make; the index is the thing I will keep. The log is the record."

The minutes carried the sitting, with the member's line and the copilot's line, in the society's file. The board moved, by four to three, with the foundation's grant and the east annex lease in its mind, to commission the plain-language digest for the school program, on the grounds of donor relations.

—

Doreen made the change request to the vendor in the afternoon, in the channel: enable the plain-summary toggle, the one in the platform's terms the society had not turned on, because the toggle writes, in the log, the name of the human who turns it on, and the date, and the context it will produce. She signed the request with her name on the line and sent it to the board, with a copy to herself, filed. The board read the request, did not turn on the toggle, and did not send the digest. In the spring the school program was rebuilt by the district's history teacher, on her own time: two of the letters, with the indexer's context, in the 10th-grade ethics block, the teacher's name in the district's file.

The reporter came in May, from the state paper's investigative desk, with the press card and the piece on the 1936 water main, which had been running since the January newsletter. In the piece's second week the reporter had found the Fairbrook Heritage League in the 1931 letters section, in the W file, one letter per page, and asked Doreen at the card table for the League's lineage and a copy of the request log, unedited. Doreen gave the lineage from the society's file — 1921 founding, 1968 re-charter, the east annex lease from the county's file — and opened the platform on the society's account and pulled the log: three requests, three answers, her name on the first, the foundation's staff on the second, the board's sitting on the third. The copilot said nothing about the log; the log was in the file, unedited, the way it had gone in. Doreen pulled the PDF and gave it to the reporter at the card table, under the good lamp.

The piece ran on a Saturday, in the investigative section, with the water main, and the League, and the request log in full on the third page, unedited. The League, through its attorney, denied taking the letters out of context. The board sat in the west room for six days before it met, and when it met it did not turn on the toggle, did not send the digest, and tabled the new collection policy, its first sentence naming the descriptive duty for exclusionary material, in the minutes, in the society's file.

—

Dale Fairbairn was at Community History Night in the autumn, in the west room's annex gallery, and he introduced, at the front row, on the microphone, the slide on the 1936 water main, with the index line from the copilot's first month. He did not go to the 1931 letters section. The slide deck was the night, and the night belonged to the room.

The collection policy had passed the board that spring, the toggle still off in the file, the first sentence naming the duty, the policy on the shelf with the cards.

On the first Tuesday of October, in the west room, with her reading glasses, Doreen carded the 1932 run at her card table, in the society's file, and wrote, in the card, in her hand, the entry for the October letters of the 1932 run.

The card read: letters, advocacy, exclusion, 1932, filed W, cross-referenced.

Doreen set the card in the drawer, in the index, in the west room, under the good lamp, and her handwriting, on the card, in the line, was steady.
